Driver Board Build

Where every part sits on the board and which hole every wire goes in. Companion to Circuit Diagram Rev. 2, which has the schematic; this one is what to do with a soldering iron in your hand.

How it goes together

Every part on the bench, and what it joins to. Three power domains, and the only thing crossing between the motor side and the display side is WiFi.

The three domains share exactly one point of contact: the Pico's GND goes to the power board's ground rail, which is the star point. That single wire is what lets the drivers read STEP and DIR against the same ground the Pico drives them from. The display column shares nothing at all.

Two things to buy that are easy to miss

Female headers, 6 × 2 × 8-pin. Your drivers already have their male pins soldered on, pointing down. If you solder those straight into the board the driver is there for good, and a blown A4988 means desoldering 16 pins next to a motor connector. Sockets cost a couple of dollars and make a dead driver a ten-second swap.

More male header. Your two 20-pin strips are exactly the Pico and nothing else. The motor connections want 6 × 4-pin, and the ribbon between boards wants 2 × 13-pin.

Check the silkscreen before you set any current

Red carriers are usually A4988, but DRV8825s exist in red too and the maths is different: A4988 is Imax = Vref ÷ (8 × Rs), DRV8825 is Imax = 2 × Vref. A DRV8825 also labels its microstep pins M0–M2 instead of MS1–MS3. The wiring below is identical either way; only the Vref number changes.

Three boards

Splitting the build in three keeps the high-current loop away from the Pico, gives you one star ground instead of several, and means a mistake on one board doesn't mean rebuilding the others.

Power board 12 V

Small board. Barrel-jack adapter, five 100 µF caps in parallel, and the star ground. Everything 12 V enters and leaves here.

Logic board 3V3

Large board. The Pico, the 3.3 V rail it feeds, and the thirteen signal wires out. No 12 V on this board at all.

Driver board(s) STEP/DIR

One large board if yours has 53+ rows, otherwise two with three drivers each. Drivers, their caps, and the motor headers.

Reading a solder breadboard

Your EPLZON boards are wired like a breadboard, not like plain perfboard. That is good news: most of the connections are made by the board itself.

  • Four rails run the length of the board, two down each long edge. Every hole in one rail is the same node. These carry power.
  • The middle is rows of five linked holes — columns a b c d e on the left of the centre gutter, f g h i j on the right. Each group of five is one node. The left five and the right five of the same row are not connected.
  • The gutter is 0.3 in, so a chip straddling it lands with its two pin rows in different banks. That's how the drivers and the Pico mount.

Count your rows before you cut anything

Each driver takes 8 rows, and this layout leaves 1 blank between them, so six drivers need 53 rows and three need 26. Count the numbered rows down one bank of your board:

  • 63 rows (the 830-point size) — all six drivers on one board.
  • 30 rows (the 400-point size) — three per board, use two boards.

Everything below gives row numbers for both.

Capacitor polarity

The stripe down the can marks the negative leg, and the negative leg is the short one. Backwards across 12 V, an electrolytic vents — loudly, and sometimes on fire. Check all eleven twice before the supply ever goes in.

Logic board (the Pico)

Solder the two 20-pin strips into the Pico so the pins point down, then mount it across the gutter with the USB socket at row 1. Its pin rows are 0.7 in apart, so the left row lands in column b and the right row in column g.

Physical pin 1 is the top-left corner next to the USB socket. Down the left edge is pins 1–20, so row = pin number. Up the right edge is pins 21–40, so row = 41 − pin.

Solder the wires before you fit the Pico

The Pico's body is 21 mm wide and overhangs columns a and h by about a millimetre. The holes stay usable, but not comfortably with the board in place. Run all fifteen wires below into the bare board first, then drop the Pico in last.

The Pico's 3.3 V regulator supplies 300 mA. Six A4988 VDD pins draw well under 100 mA between them, so one Pico comfortably powers all six drivers' logic.

Driver board, hole by hole

Each driver straddles the gutter with its pin rows in columns c and g — 0.6 in apart, which is what the carrier is. That leaves a and b free on the logic side and h, i, j free on the power side, which is where every wire goes.

Orient the driver so its ENABLE pin is at the low-numbered row and the VMOT pin is opposite it. Get this backwards and 12 V lands on the ENABLE input.

The four details that matter

  • The bridge in column a is one piece of solid wire soldered into five consecutive holes, rows N+1 to N+5, then carried on to the +3V3 rail. It ties MS1, MS2, MS3, RESET and SLEEP high in one go: all three microstep pins high is 1/16 stepping, and RESET and SLEEP high is what wakes the driver up. Keep it clear of row N and row N+6, which are ENABLE and STEP.
  • The capacitor sits in column h, positive leg in row N (VMOT) and negative in row N+1 (GND) — right at the pins, which is the point of it. If its legs are 3.5 mm apart rather than 2.54, bend them gently to reach.
  • VDD gets its own wire. 3.3 V is needed on both sides of the driver, and the rails can't be in two places, so run one wire from column i of row N+6 round the end of the driver to the +3V3 rail. One per driver.
  • The motor header goes in column j, rows N+2 to N+5, in the order GRN, GRN/WHT, RED, RED/WHT top to bottom. Both motor types use the same colours. Confirm with a meter before powering: the two leads of one coil read about 1.5 Ω to each other and open to the other coil.

ENABLE is a single Pico pin feeding six driver inputs. Take one wire from the logic board to the first driver board and daisy-chain it to the second, rather than running two long wires from the same hole.

The 12 V supply is tighter than it looks

At 1 A per coil into roughly 1.5 Ω windings, twelve coils dissipate about 18 W, which the choppers pull from 12 V as roughly 1.5 A before driver losses. A 2 A supply covers that, but not with much room. It's fine in practice because the hands move rarely and seldom together — just don't be surprised if a simultaneous six-hand sweep at full current sags the rail. Setting Vref for 0.8 A instead of 1.0 removes the question entirely, and a clock hand needs nothing like 1 A.

Solder order

This sequence exists so that every stage can be tested before the next one can damage anything. Don't reorder it.

  1. Power board. Barrel adapter, then the five bulk caps. Check polarity on all five. Don't plug the supply in.
  2. Logic board, wires first. All thirteen signal wires into column b, the 3V3 rail wire, the GND wire. Then the Pico's headers, then the Pico.
  3. Test the Pico alone. USB to the Pi, flash the firmware, confirm it enumerates. Nothing else is connected yet.
  4. Driver board, everything except the drivers. Rail wires, the column-a bridges, the VDD wires, the caps, the motor headers, the sockets if you bought them.
  5. Continuity check with the board bare. Meter from each rail to each hole it should reach, and — more important — check that +12V and GND are open to each other, and that +3V3 and GND are open to each other. This is the cheapest five minutes in the build.
  6. Fit the drivers. Logic power only. No 12 V, no motors. Confirm 3.3 V on every VDD pin and 0 V on every VMOT.
  7. Set Vref on each driver — see below. Still no 12 V to the motors and no motors connected.
  8. Power off. Connect 12 V, motors still unplugged. Confirm 12 V at each VMOT. Leave it a minute and feel for anything warm.
  9. Power off. Connect one motor. Power on, send a few steps, watch it turn. Then add the rest one at a time, powering down between each.

Never

  • Never plug or unplug a motor with 12 V on. The coil's collapsing field has nowhere to go and it destroys the driver. This is the single most common way these boards die.
  • Never power 12 V before setting Vref. The pot ships at an arbitrary position and can be at full current.
  • Never fit a driver backwards. ENABLE at the low row, VMOT opposite.
  • Never tie Pico pin 37 (3V3_EN) to anything. It shuts the regulator off.

Setting the current limit

Logic power on, motor supply off, motor disconnected. Measure between the trim pot's metal screw and GND, and turn the pot until you read the figure for your board's sense resistor. Read the marking on the resistor next to the pot.

A4988 only. A DRV8825 uses Imax = 2 × Vref, so 0.8 A is 0.40 V whatever the sense resistor.

The 23HS18 is rated 2.0 A and the 17HS13 1.5 A, so either figure leaves headroom. Start at 0.8 A: a clock hand needs almost no torque, the drivers stay cool, and the 12 V supply stops being marginal. Stick a heatsink on each driver anyway.

Display nodes

Six self-contained ESP32 + GC9A01 pairs, each on its own USB power, with no wire to the motor side at all. The Pi reaches them over WiFi. Nothing here is soldered — it's all female-to-female dupont between two pin headers.
