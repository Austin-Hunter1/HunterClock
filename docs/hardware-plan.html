System Architecture

How a location becomes a hand movement

Four stages, each independently testable: location sources feed a broker, the broker feeds the controller, and the controller drives two kinds of hardware that don't talk to each other directly. All 6 family members carry Apple devices, so Find My is the primary source — Home Assistant's iCloud integration is known to be occasionally flaky (Apple rate-limits and 2FA re-prompts break it every few weeks), so keep OwnTracks installed as a silent fallback on at least Becky's, Evan's, and Elijah's phones, the three that move most and would be missed first if Find My drops.

The Load-Bearing Decision

One face with six hands, or six faces

The show clock pivots every hand from one center point — which means concentric shafts. That's the first thing this plan had to rule in or out, because it caps how far the design can scale.

Concentric shaft stack Selected

  • Authentic single-dial look, closest to the reference — and now that the original movement is being gutted entirely, the true center pivot and the full trunk cavity are both available
  • 6 hollow-shaft motors daisy-chained along the depth axis, each one's bore wide enough for every shaft behind it to pass through
  • Off-the-shelf hollow-shaft bore tops out around 10-11mm at NEMA17 — the outer 2 stages need to step up to NEMA23 to clear the cascade
  • One jam anywhere in the stack can bind every hand behind it — accepted, since reliability mattered less than the authentic look

Six independent gauges

  • Each person gets their own small dial, stepper, and pointer — mechanically identical to the software prototype's clock face, just built six times
  • A failed or offline motor affects one person, not the whole clock
  • Off-the-shelf 28BYJ-48 + ULN2003 pairs, no custom shafts
  • Considered and costed (see below), but set aside in favor of the single-pivot look

The plan below builds around a single 6-shaft hub at the original dial's center, with two side-mounted panels handling the moving-portrait display for all 6 hands.

Verify Fit

Will the hub actually fit inside this cabinet?

From the photo, this is a Howard Miller triple-weight, moon-phase case — the same family as models like the Robinson (23"W × 13.5"D) and Stewart (23"W × 14"D), up through larger ones like the J.H. Miller (31"W × 20.75"D). Exterior depth across that range runs roughly 13.5"–20.75" (343–527mm).

The math The hub's estimated depth is ~300–320mm (11.8"–12.6"). Against a 13.5"–14" case — the shallower, more common end of this family — that's tight: subtract front and back panel thickness and the hood alone likely doesn't clear it. That's not fatal, since the whole trunk is already being gutted and is typically as deep or deeper than the hood (it has to be, to clear the pendulum's swing) — the motor chain can run down through the seat-board opening into the trunk rather than staying entirely behind the dial plate. But "likely fits if it spills into the trunk" isn't the same as confirmed, and the hub is the single largest, least-returnable line in the budget.

Three measurements settle it. The motors are already ordered (StepperOnline order 330093), so these decide whether the stack fits as planned or needs adapting:

1. Hood depth
From the back of the dial plate to the interior back panel, measured where the dial currently sits.
2. Trunk depth
Interior front-to-back depth of the gutted trunk cavity below the seat board, where the chain would continue if it doesn't fit in the hood alone.
3. Narrowest interior width
The tightest point the motor chain has to pass through — NEMA23's 57mm (2.24") square frame is the widest single obstacle in the stack.

How The Decision Was Costed

Six independent gauges vs. a concentric hub

A hollow-shaft concentric hub is mechanically real, but the shafts have to nest, and each motor's bore has to be wide enough for every shaft behind it to pass through. That cascade is what separates a 3-shaft chain from a 6-shaft one: 3 levels fit comfortably inside a single NEMA17 frame's ~10mm bore ceiling; 6 levels don't, and force the front stages up to a bulkier NEMA23 frame. Decision — single 6-shaft hub The original movement, weights, and pendulum are coming out entirely, which removed the depth objection to the single hub: instead of a few inches behind the dial plate, the whole trunk cavity (previously filled by the weight-drop channels) is available, so all three layouts fit. With depth off the table, the remaining trade was cost (~75% more than independent gauges, once the whole system is priced — see below) against failure isolation (a jam anywhere in the hub's stack can affect all 6 hands, versus at most 3 in a two-chain layout, versus 1 in independent gauges) — and the single-pivot look won. Levels 4–6 (which additionally have to pass through Motor 3's 4mm bore) drop to 1/8", 3/32", and 1/16" (3.2mm, 2.4mm, 1.6mm OD) — also a clean run of adjacent K&S steps, comfortably clear of 4mm. Every other interface is a standard consecutive K&S pair. Level 6 still needs the 2-kit length margin noted above.

Interconnect Budget

Why the motor logic lives on its own microcontroller

Each A4988 driver needs a STEP and a DIR pin, so six bipolar steppers need 12 GPIO lines with precise, jitter-free pulse timing — six simultaneous step streams is more than a general-purpose CPU core can guarantee while also handling MQTT and display traffic.

Recommendation Offload all 6 motors to a Raspberry Pi Pico, using the RP2040's PIO state machines to generate each motor's step pulses in hardware — one PIO program per motor, running independently of whatever the Pico's two CPU cores are doing. The Pi sends one short command per person ({person, target_step}) over USB serial; the Pico owns all real-time step timing. The 6 ESP32 display nodes need no wiring back to the Pi at all — they join the same WiFi network and subscribe to MQTT topics like clock/display/evan directly.

This also isolates failure domains cleanly: a Pico crash stalls the hands but not the displays or the location pipeline; a WiFi hiccup stalls one display node, not the motors. Within the hub itself, a jam in one shaft can still drag its neighbors — that's the trade-off the single-pivot look was chosen over. Exact GPIO pin assignments for all 6 STEP/DIR pairs are in the circuit diagram.

Build Sequence

✓ Complete — birthday_clock.m

1

Single-channel proof of concept

One NEMA17 hollow-shaft stepper + one ESP32/AITRIP display + MQTT round trip, with Find My publishing one real family member's GPS through Home Assistant. Proves the pipeline and the PIO step-generation approach before cutting any tubing. Also the moment to measure the actual cabinet (see "Verify Fit" above) before the hub is built.

2

Hub fabrication & six-channel actuation

Machine or source the 6-level graduated tube stack, fit bearings between levels, mount all 6 motors nose-to-tail in the gutted trunk. Pico firmware drives all 6 from serial commands; Pi-side controller maps each MQTT topic to a person and a target angle.

3

Display nodes & portraits

6 ESP32 nodes on WiFi, each looping a real recorded clip of its person (cropped to a circle, resized to 240×240, re-encoded as a short frame sequence) and switching state (home / traveling / unknown) on MQTT command.

4

Face rebuild & mounting

Original movement, weights, and pendulum removed. New chapter ring machined for the log-distance scale, reusing the dial's original brass spandrels and corner filigree; hub and display bezels mounted in the gutted trunk.

5

Integration & polish

Home Assistant wired in for aggregation, offline/stale-location handling, and a calibration pass on the log-scale constants for each family member's actual travel range.
